Why real data matters

A model trained on simulated students collapsed on real ones

Topic accuracy fell from 74% to 50.9% the moment real students arrived.

Real response times are far more variable than the simulator assumed, so ordinary students looked slow.

Training on real EdNet logs recovered 18.7 and 28.5 points.
This is the key lesson of the project. Stage one used simulated students and looked great on paper. On real students it fell apart, which is domain shift. That is why the final model is trained only on real logs.

Real students have no hidden mastery value, so the label is their future: accuracy on the next forty questions of the same part. Features only use the past. Notice how imbalanced pacing is, eighty-four percent balanced. That comes back on the pacing results slide.

Logistic regression captures smooth trends, the random forest captures thresholds and interactions. Soft voting averages their probabilities. Every score is measured on students the model never saw, because one student contributes many rows.

When five very different models and a stronger boosting model all stop at the same accuracy, the algorithm is not the bottleneck, the information in the data is. Labels are noisy: accuracy over forty questions moves about eight points by chance.

Skipping dominates: students who do not attempt questions tend to be weak. The difficulty-adjusted score is third, which justifies engineering it. For pacing, a student's usual speed is the best predictor, so pacing behaves like a personal trait.

Be honest here. Ninety percent sounds great, but guessing balanced every time already gets eighty-nine. Balanced accuracy is the fair measure: 51.5 against 33.3 for guessing. The fix is better data from real timed exams.
